Cashier → merchant service · Signed invoice → cashier

An explanatory walkthrough, not a live transaction. The customer-to-cashier handoff is encrypted; the cashier-to-service connection uses HTTPS and signed requests.

Read all six steps
  1. Request: an approved cashier requests an invoice. The merchant service signs the receiving identity, amount-bearing session, cashier, network and expiry.
  2. Check: the customer scans the signed request. Their wallet verifies the signature and shows the merchant and amount.
  3. Pay: the customer creates a BRC-29 output for the merchant and sends the payment through the encrypted nearby transport.
  4. Relay: the cashier saves the exact received transaction, then forwards it to the online merchant service.
  5. Accept: the service checks authorization, invoice binding, amount, destination and transaction evidence through the wallet, then checks acceptance status.
  6. Confirm: only a matching merchant-signed receipt makes the cashier sale show Paid. A radio acknowledgment follows merchant confirmation when the connection remains available.
The important distinction

The phone you talk to
isn’t the wallet you pay.

A nearby connection and a payment destination serve different purposes. We keep them separate so a cashier can help take a payment without becoming the recipient.

01

Encrypt the handoff to the cashier.

The QR-paired nearby session protects data between the customer’s and cashier’s devices. Existing nearby radios or an encrypted animated QR carry the payment.

02

Derive the output for the merchant.

BRC-29 is the wallet payment protocol used to create the merchant’s receiving output. The customer uses the merchant’s public identity, not the cashier’s identity, as the counterparty.

03

Let the merchant validate the destination.

The merchant signing service has the private derivation capability needed to check the expected output. The cashier does not have that capability.

Two relationships, one checkout
Customer deviceCashier device

The paired devices exchange the payment data. A secure handoff does not change the recipient.

Customer walletMerchant wallet

The money is directed to the business. It does not pass through the cashier’s personal wallet.

The merchant’s private key stays on the merchant signing service. Cloud storage is a separate component; storage alone cannot derive the receiving keys.

Offline means one side, not every side

No signal for the customer?
That’s the idea.

A customer with a funded, prepared wallet can create and hand over the payment locally. The cashier is the bridge to the online merchant service.

If the cashier loses service access or the merchant service goes down, the sale cannot be confirmed. A received payment stays pending until the merchant can validate it.

A timeout is an uncertain result. Save the original payment, check its status, and retry the same transaction against the same invoice—do not ask the customer to pay again.

Why this architecture

Built around the way
a small shop actually works.

Owners, staff and customers need different things. The design makes those boundaries explicit.

The choiceWhat it gives youThe tradeoff

A dedicated merchant app

Merchant enrollment, cashier approvals and sale recovery have a home without adding a POS mode to every customer’s wallet.

A second app to operate, with shared wallet and nearby-transport components underneath.

Individual cashier identities

Staff can be approved and revoked without distributing the business’s private key. Requests are bound to the authorized cashier.

Enrollment is a deliberate owner task. Revocation also blocks cashier retries; outstanding sales may need merchant intervention.

An online merchant authority

The service can derive the expected receiving output and validate the transaction before issuing an acceptance receipt.

The business needs an available, securely operated signing service and cloud wallet. Full merchant-offline acceptance is outside this flow.

Saved transactions and signed receipts

The same submitted payment can be checked and retried after a lost response. A different transaction cannot reuse a submitted invoice.

Uncertain results stay pending. Wallet acceptance is not a promise that the payment has already been mined into a block.

Go as the primary service

The gateway builds on Go Wallet Toolbox signing, wallet acceptance and authenticated cloud storage. The app uses TypeScript and Expo.

The first service runs one process per merchant with durable state. Multiple replicas need transactional storage and coordinated locking.

The customer wallet must include support for signed POS requests. A local-currency total must never be entered as if it were a satoshi amount.

The EPOS integration should remove retyping, not add another total.

The intended adapter sends the amount, currency and sale reference from the existing till into the payment flow, then returns the merchant-confirmed result to that same sale. A QR can carry the handoff if the EPOS supports it; scanning a total alone does not reconcile the order. Square and Clover are candidates to investigate with local US merchants. The first partner will be chosen for actual merchant use and integration access, not an assumed global market-share ranking.

A few fair questions

The details that matter
before the first sale.

Does the cashier ever receive the business’s money?

The customer creates a receiving output for the merchant’s BRC-29 identity. The cashier relays the payment data and uses their own identity for authorization. This flow does not route the funds through the cashier’s wallet.

Is the cloud storage provider the merchant signing wallet?

They are separate roles. The Go gateway operates the merchant signing wallet and connects to authenticated cloud storage. Storage alone does not have the private derivation capability needed for merchant destination checks. Deploy and protect the signing identity as a merchant credential.

What happens if the response disappears after payment?

The cashier app and service preserve the exact submitted transaction. Check status and retry that transaction against its invoice. If acceptance already produced a receipt, the service returns the stored receipt. Do not treat a timeout as rejection or request another payment. Revoked-cashier recovery may require merchant intervention.

Does “Paid” mean a mined blockchain confirmation?

No. It means the merchant service has validated the transaction and its destination and amount, obtained wallet acceptance, and checked the action status before issuing a signed receipt. The first version accepts the wallet’s unproven or completed states; sending and failed states do not produce a paid receipt. This is the wallet’s network-acceptance policy, not block-confirmed finality.

Will the offline customer always get the final receipt?

Not if the original nearby connection has already timed out. The cashier can resolve the persisted sale, but a dedicated receipt QR or reconnection flow is still planned. A radio acknowledgment is sent after merchant confirmation while the session remains available; the animated QR fallback retains the wallet’s manual handoff confirmation behavior.

Can this plug straight into my existing EPOS?

Not in this version. The first app accepts manual whole-satoshi entry. An EPOS adapter must both transfer the correct sale total and reconcile the confirmed payment with the till. Provider APIs, external-payment rules, device support and approval requirements need validation before promising a plugin.

Does the customer need the Merchant app too?

No. The customer uses BSV Wallet with support for the signed merchant request. Merchant and cashier setup stay in BSV Merchant. Older customer wallet builds will need an update to recognize these POS codes.
